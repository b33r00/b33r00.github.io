Roadmap

Penetration Testing Development Path

This roadmap is not a certificate shopping list and not an HR compliance ritual. The objective is specialization and real technical understanding: the ability to explain a system, break it, rebuild it, and then break it again on purpose — with intent, not superstition. Hands-on practice matters only if it produces proof: artifacts, writeups, tools, and clean reasoning. Anything that cannot be reproduced, defended, or explained is not a skill; it’s a coincidence dressed up as confidence.

The path is depth-first: Linux → networking → programming & logic → exploitation methodology → realistic environments. Everything else is downstream. “Pentesting” is not a topic you memorize. It is an epistemic discipline: you generate hypotheses, you instrument reality, you kill what fails, you keep what survives.

I run this with a strict 80/20 rule: 80% practice, 20% theory. Theory is there to sharpen the model, not to replace contact with reality. If the week doesn’t produce artifacts — configs, notes, scripts, writeups, reproducible labs — it didn’t produce progress.

Phase 0 — Linux first, because Linux is the spine

System knowledge is just as critical, especially on Linux. It is not enough to “know Debian” or to be comfortable with a desktop environment. The goal here is to inhabit it. Not as a user, but as someone who can reason about state, privilege, services, failure modes, and recovery without turning reinstall into a coping mechanism. You build competence by manufacturing friction: minimal installs, manual configuration, and systems you can break and recover repeatedly. The point is to stop treating the OS as a black box and start treating it as a machine whose behavior you can predict, explain, and deliberately manipulate.

Core focus is not distro tribalism, but mechanics:

  • processes, permissions, capabilities, namespaces, cgroups
  • filesystems, mounts, overlays, device nodes
  • init, services, logging, boot chain (what exists before you get a shell)
  • the host networking stack (sockets, routes, resolvers, firewall rules)

You don’t “learn” these by reading. You learn them by doing something that should work, watching it not work, and refusing to accept mystery as an answer. Arch/Artix minimal + i3 is a constraint, not an aesthetic. Later, Gentoo is a forcing function: it takes away your ability to hide behind defaults. Ricing is irrelevant visually; it matters only insofar as it forces you to understand what you changed. If your system breaks and you can’t explain exactly why, you don’t “have a setup,” you have a fragile illusion.

Phase 1 — Networking without memorization

Networking learned as trivia is useless. Networking learned as mechanics becomes leverage. Study serious material (university-level PDFs are fine) to understand what’s under the hood, then prove it in configuration and observation. The OSI model is not a poster; it’s a map for debugging. The goal is not to recite layers, but to predict behavior when the environment becomes hostile: segmentation, policy, state, and the quiet places where assumptions leak.

Keep the focus on behaviors you can demonstrate:

  • routing, switching, ARP, DNS, DHCP
  • VLANs and segmentation as real constraints (and how they fail)
  • NAT, firewall policy, statefulness, packet flow

Cisco Packet Tracer matters because it forces manual configuration. Home lab networking matters because it forces consequences. The feedback loop is the point: you build a network, it behaves unexpectedly, you instrument it, you correct the model in your head. That correction is the skill. If you can’t explain why lateral movement was possible, you didn’t “find a path,” you stumbled into one.

Phase 2 — Programming and logic as weapons

A pentester who can only glue tools together is limited by other people’s imagination. Programming is not optional if you want depth. You need speed, yes — but you also need the ability to reason at the level where exploitation becomes intelligible. The difference between “I ran a tool” and “I understood what happened” is usually programming and logic, not another cheat sheet.

Automation layer: Bash and Python, because repetition is a tax and automation is refusing to pay it. Systems layer: C/C++ because memory, undefined behavior, and runtime realities are where a lot of “magic” turns into engineering. Logic layer: because the real meta-skill is the ability to hold complex state and not panic when the path isn’t visible yet — the ability to remain cold while the problem remains messy.

Minimal “non-negotiables” here:

  • Bash for environment control and pipeline thinking
  • Python for tooling, parsing, orchestration, PoC shaping
  • C/C++ for systems-level reasoning (what the compiler actually did, what memory actually is)

And then the part most people skip: deliberate logic training. Hard problems, RE of small programs, reading code you didn’t author, writing small test programs to validate hypotheses. This is how you stop outsourcing your thinking to “known techniques” and start generating your own. Chess and reading also help — not because it’s romantic, but because attention and pattern discipline are transferable. Consider them mental deadlifts; the joke is that it looks like procrastination to people who don’t understand compounding, but it’s the same discipline: consistent pressure, slow accumulation, sudden payoff.

Phase 3 — Methodology over random hacking

Once Linux + networking + programming are not shallow, exploitation stops being roulette. Penetration testing becomes a structured lifecycle: recon → enumeration → initial access → privilege escalation → lateral movement → impact validation → clean, reproducible proof. The method is not bureaucracy; it’s anti-delusion. It forces you to justify actions with evidence instead of vibes, and it keeps you from confusing movement with progress.

Keep one small checklist, but treat it as scaffolding, not authority. The real deliverable is a personal methodology you actually follow, plus post-mortems: what you missed, what fooled you, what you corrected. If you can’t describe your own failure modes, you’re not improving — you’re just repeating.

Phase 4 — Platforms used with intent

Platforms are training instruments, not identity. Use them by role:

  • TryHackMe for structured ramps and coverage
  • PortSwigger Web Security Academy for web depth and precision
  • HackingHub for high-repetition web exploitation drills
  • OverTheWire to harden Linux fundamentals and manual habits
  • VulnLab for realistic scenarios and enterprise flavor
  • HTB Academy + Hack The Box for advanced practice and grinding under ambiguity

Machines should be done regularly with long sessions. Struggle is part of the process; it’s where your model gets stress-tested. Document reasoning, not just solutions. A writeup that only lists steps is a diary; a writeup that exposes thinking is evidence. The discipline is not “solve more boxes,” it is to train the exact behaviors you will need under uncertainty:

  • multiple hours a day (when possible)
  • struggle is part of the process
  • write down solutions and thought processes

Phase 5 — Web security beyond the greatest hits

Classic vulnerabilities matter, but they’re not the finish line. Don’t just name them; understand where they live and why they work: SQL injection, XSS, CSRF, file upload bypasses, SSTI, authentication issues. Depth means understanding contexts, sinks, encodings, framework behavior — payload crafting as engineering, not copy/paste. If your approach is “try payloads until something pops,” you’re not testing; you’re gambling.

Business logic is where real impact often hides in plain sight, because it’s not “a bug,” it’s a broken model of reality. This is why it deserves more than a mention: you’re not exploiting a parser, you’re exploiting assumptions.

  • workflow abuse (using the system in an order it didn’t anticipate)
  • authorization bypass (being someone you’re not allowed to be)
  • payment manipulation (numbers, states, and trust boundaries)
  • broken verification processes (the illusion of checks that don’t actually bind)

Treat business logic like a chess position: you don’t look for a single tactic, you look for the structure that makes tactics possible.

Phase 6 — Active Directory as enterprise reality

AD is core because enterprise is core. Treat it as a map, not a bag of tricks. The point is chaining and position, not single techniques. You learn it properly by building multiple labs with different misconfigurations until “enterprise compromise” stops being a story and becomes a navigable structure.

Core technique surface (learned as components of paths, not party tricks):

  • Kerberoasting, AS-REP roasting
  • NTLM relay
  • pass-the-hash / pass-the-ticket
  • abusing misconfigured services, permissions, delegation
  • attack path analysis with BloodHound

The didactic rule is simple: every technique you learn must be embedded into at least one reproducible chain. If you can’t explain where it sits in an attack path, it’s trivia. If you can’t reproduce it, it’s mythology.

Phase 7 — Cloud (mentioned, not worshipped)

You don’t need to become a cloud engineer, but you do need baseline literacy because hybrids are common. Understand common attack surfaces (IAM misconfigs, exposed storage, metadata abuse, typical privilege escalation paths) well enough that you’re not blind during assessments. Keep it concise; treat it as “what to check and why,” not an identity. The goal is competence, not cosplay.

Phase 8 — Proof: writeups and reporting

Writeups are not trophies; they are evidence. Not many writeups — valuable ones. The goal is not to show that a box was solved, but to show how it was approached: enumeration choices, failed hypotheses, pivots, why the final path worked. This is where you make your thinking legible, and legibility is the difference between “trust me” and “here is the proof.”

Proof is not limited to writeups. Home lab projects are proof too — often stronger proof — because they demonstrate systems thinking: building environments, introducing realistic constraints, and then breaking them deliberately. Documented lab builds (AD domains, segmented networks, misconfig sets, vulnerable services) plus reproducible configs, diagrams, and attack-path notes (foothold → escalation → lateral movement → impact) are not “learning.” They are evidence.

Pair that with professional reporting skills: clear reproduction steps, technical detail, business impact framing, prioritized remediation. This is what separates “I can hack” from “I can deliver value.” The report is not an afterthought; it is the interface between technical truth and action.

Signal and stress-testing (community without cringe)

Real-life experience and community involvement matter when they function as stress-tests and signal, not as virtue badges. Use realistic scenarios (CyberDefenders) to bridge the gap between labs and real environments. Use events (BSides, Hacktivity, and similar conferences) as exposure to practitioners and real narratives of failure. Use onsite and online CTFs as time-pressure training and collaboration practice. The value is not “participation.” The value is what it does to your thinking under constraints.

Meta — attacker mindset, the disciplined kind

The attacker mindset is not roleplay and not paperwork. It’s disciplined imagination: identify high-value assets, chain weaknesses into realistic paths, prioritize by impact, keep thinking when the obvious path is blocked. If you want shorthand: think less “checklist compliance,” more “Elliot with instrumentation.” The paranoia must be testable. Out-of-the-box thinking is only useful if you can bring it back into the box as a reproducible method.
